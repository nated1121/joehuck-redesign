## Strip 3: Article

**Photo:** `site/assets/img/jobs/panel-connections.jpg`  
**Alt text:** Tightening connections inside an electrical panel during an inspection

**Signs box**

- **H2:** Good Times to Schedule a Check
- Bulleted list:

> - You just bought a home, or you're about to sell one
> - The house is 25 or more years old and hasn't been looked at
> - You've added large appliances, an EV or a hot tub
> - After a storm, flood or lightning strike nearby
> - Before a remodel, so you know what you're working with

**Section 1**

- **H2:** What We Check
- Paragraph:

> We open the panel and look at the breakers, the bus bar, and the main, neutral and ground connections, and tighten terminations where needed. We check for overheating, corrosion and double-tapped breakers. We test every GFCI and AFCI, check a sample of outlets for polarity and grounding, and look at exposed wiring in the basement, attic and garage.

- Paragraph:

> We also test your smoke and carbon monoxide alarms and note any that are missing or past their replacement date. You get a written list of what we found, sorted into safety items, code items and nice-to-haves, with prices.

- Links in this section:
  - Link **"smoke and carbon monoxide alarms"** → Electrical Inspections & Maintenance page

**Section 2**

- **H2:** Small Business Maintenance
- Paragraph:

> For small offices and shops, we check panels, emergency and exit lighting, outdoor lighting, and dedicated circuits for equipment. We can schedule regular visits so problems are caught between busy seasons.


**Section 3**

- **H2:** What Happens Next
- Paragraph:

> Most visits end with nothing urgent and a few small fixes we can take care of the same day. If we find something bigger, like a panel with a known failure history or ungrounded circuits, we explain the options and give you a written price. If you're preparing for a sale, the code corrections section below covers the items inspectors flag most often. Adding a whole-house surge protector is a common upgrade after a maintenance check.

- Links in this section:
  - Link **"whole-house surge protector"** → Whole House Surge Protection page

**Section 4**

- **H2:** For Older Homes and New Owners
- Paragraph:

> In homes more than 40 years old, we pay extra attention to aluminum or cloth-insulated wiring, panels with known failure histories, and past additions that may not have been done to code. For new homeowners, a maintenance visit is a good way to learn where the panel is, what each circuit feeds and how to reset GFCIs and AFCIs. We'll label anything that isn't clearly marked while we're there.


**Section 5**

- **H2:** Code Corrections After a Home Inspection
- Paragraph:

> If you're buying or selling, the inspector's report usually lists electrical items: double-tapped breakers, missing GFCIs, open junction boxes, ungrounded outlets or a panel with a known failure history. We fix them, pull a permit where one is needed and give you documentation for the buyer, seller or lender. Buyers can have us review the report before their negotiation deadline and get real prices to work with.


**Section 6**

- **H2:** Smoke and Carbon Monoxide Alarms
- Paragraph:

> Smoke alarms last about ten years, and many CO alarms less. We install hardwired, interconnected smoke and CO alarms with battery backup where code requires them, replace units past their date, and fix alarms that chirp or go off for no reason. In older homes we fish the new wiring through finished walls with as little patching as possible.


**Section 7**

- **H2:** Maintenance Around Yardley
- Paragraph:

> We do inspections for new homeowners in Newtown and Morrisville, and maintenance for small businesses in Langhorne.

- **H3:** How often should my home's electrical system be inspected?

> For most homes, every few years, and any time you buy a house, add major loads or notice a problem.

- **H3:** What's the most common thing you find?

> GFCI outlets that no longer trip when tested, smoke alarms past their ten-year life, and loose connections in the panel.

- **H3:** Is this the same as a home inspection?

> No. A home inspector looks at the whole house; we go deeper into the electrical system and can fix what we find.
